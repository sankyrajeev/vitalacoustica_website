"Line array" has become shorthand for "professional" in a lot of event briefs. Sometimes that is exactly right. Sometimes a well-chosen pair of point-source speakers would sound better, rig faster and cost less. The difference is not size or brand. It is how each design spreads sound through a space.

## What a point-source speaker does

A point-source loudspeaker behaves, from a distance, as if all its sound comes from one spot. Its energy spreads out in an expanding shape, horizontally and vertically, with a coverage pattern set by the horn or waveguide (for example, 90 degrees wide by 40 degrees high).

Because the sound spreads in every direction it is aimed, its level drops by roughly **6 dB each time the distance doubles**. A listener at 4 metres hears it about 12 dB louder than a listener at 16 metres. In a small or medium room that difference is manageable. In a deep room it means the front is too loud or the back is too quiet.

## What a line array does

A line array stacks several identical elements vertically so that, across much of the frequency range, they combine into a single, coherent wavefront. In the vertical plane that wavefront is controlled very tightly; horizontally, coverage is set by each element's waveguide.

In the region close to a long array, level falls more slowly with distance than a point source does: in the idealised case about **3 dB per doubling** rather than 6. That is the origin of the claim that line arrays "throw further". The useful truth is more nuanced:

- Real arrays are finite, so this behaviour holds over a limited distance that depends on frequency and array length.
- Modern arrays are curved, with splay angles chosen box by box, so that the top of the array reaches the back of the room and the bottom covers the front. The aim is **even level from front to back**, not maximum throw.
- To control the low-mid frequencies, the array needs enough length. A very short array behaves more like a cluster of point sources.

This is why line arrays are designed with the manufacturer's prediction software, not by eye: the number of boxes, their angles and the trim height all decide how the audience area is covered.

## Side by side

| | Point source | Line array |
|---|---|---|
| Level over distance | About 6 dB lost per doubling | Less loss over the array's near field, shaped by splay angles |
| Vertical control | Set by the horn; fixed | Shaped by the number of boxes and their angles |
| Best at | Short to medium throws, small rooms, fills | Deep audience areas, large rooms, outdoors |
| Rigging | Stands, yokes or simple flying | Usually flown on rated points, with a qualified rigger |
| Design work | Aim and place | Predict, model, trim and angle |
| Set-up time | Shorter | Longer |

## When point source is the better choice

- **Small and medium rooms** where the furthest listener is not much further away than the nearest.
- **Low ceilings**, where there is no height to fly an array.
- **Speech-led events** in modest spaces, where clarity matters more than level.
- **Fill duties** in any size of system: front fills for the first rows, out fills for the sides, and delays further back.
- **Quick turnarounds**, where rigging time is limited.

Column arrays, tall and slim point sources with steerable or shaped vertical coverage, are a third option for speech in reverberant rooms such as halls and places of worship.

## When a line array earns its place

- **Deep audience areas**, where the difference between front and back would be too great for a point source.
- **Large outdoor events**, where there are no walls to help and coverage has to be shaped deliberately.
- **High-level music** across a large crowd, where consistency at the back matters as much as impact at the front.
- **Venues with suitable rigging points**, where flying the system improves sightlines and coverage.

## Three common myths

**"Line arrays are louder."** Not inherently. Their advantage is consistency over distance. A point-source system of similar size can produce similar level up close.

**"Every professional event needs one."** In a 150-person banquet room, a line array can be the wrong tool: too much rigging for too little benefit.

**"More boxes are always better."** More boxes give more control and more headroom, but only if they are designed and angled for the room. Badly aimed, they just make reflections louder.

## Most good systems are hybrids

Large systems rarely use one approach alone. A typical design might combine flown line arrays for the main audience, point-source front fills along the stage lip, out fills for the sides and delay speakers for the back of a long room, all timed and tuned to sound like one system.

## How to choose

Ask four questions about the venue:

1. **How deep is the audience area?** Compare the nearest and furthest listener.
2. **Is there height and rated rigging?** If not, the choice may be made for you.
3. **What is the programme?** Speech, background music and high-level dance music have very different needs.
4. **How much time is there?** Arrays need more time to rig, trim and tune.
