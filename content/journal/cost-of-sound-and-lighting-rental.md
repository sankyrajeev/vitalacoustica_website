Ask three companies to quote for the same event and you will often get three very different numbers. That is not always because someone is overcharging. Sound and lighting hire is mostly time, people and logistics, with the equipment itself only part of the picture. Understanding what sits behind a quote helps you compare like with like, and spend where it makes a difference.

## The equipment: scale and specification

The obvious driver is what is being hired:

- **How much coverage is needed.** A larger audience area needs more loudspeakers, more amplification and often fills and delays.
- **The tier of equipment.** Touring-grade systems from established manufacturers cost more to own, maintain and insure than entry-level gear, and they are priced accordingly.
- **The lighting design.** A handful of uplighters and a few washes is a different scale from a truss full of moving heads, LED surfaces, haze and a programmed show.
- **Control.** Consoles, playback, networking and spares are easy to overlook, but they are part of every system.

A useful question is not "what is the cheapest system?" but "what is the smallest system that does this job properly, with headroom?"

## Time: it is rarely just the event day

Most of the cost of an event happens outside the hours the audience sees.

- **Hire period.** Equipment is usually charged per day or per event. Multi-day events, rehearsals and early set-ups all add days.
- **Set-up and rehearsal.** A tuned sound system and a programmed lighting show take time to install, test and refine. Compressing that time often means more crew, or compromises.
- **Late finishes and overnight load-outs.** Many venues require the space cleared the same night. That changes crew hours and transport.

## People: the crew is part of the system

A quote that lists equipment but not people is missing something. Professional crew typically includes:

- System technicians who install, connect and test the equipment.
- A sound engineer who tunes the system and operates it during the event.
- A lighting operator or programmer, especially for cue-based shows.
- Riggers, where anything is flown.

Crew costs depend on how many people are needed, for how long, and for how many days. They are also where experience shows: the difference between a show that runs smoothly and one that does not is usually a person, not a speaker.

## Logistics: getting it there and back

- **Distance and vehicles.** Equipment travels in road cases, in trucks sized for the job.
- **Access.** Stairs, lifts, long pushes from the loading bay and restricted load-in windows all take time.
- **Venue rules.** Some venues require their own approved suppliers for rigging or power, or charge for access and parking.

## Infrastructure: power, rigging and structure

- **Power.** If the venue cannot supply enough clean, dedicated power, a generator and power distribution need to be added, along with fuel and someone to manage them.
- **Rigging.** Flying speakers or lights requires rated points, truss, hoists and qualified people.
- **Staging and structures.** Ground-supported truss, stages, risers and FOH platforms add equipment and crew.

## Complexity

Two events with similar equipment lists can still differ in effort:

- Programmed lighting cues, timecode or sync with video and music.
- Integration with other suppliers: video, stage, broadcast, the venue's own systems.
- Multiple rooms, multiple stages, or changeovers between acts.
- Redundancy: backup playback, spare microphones, spare consoles for critical moments.

## How to get a quote you can compare

The more a provider knows, the more accurate and comparable their quote. Send:

1. **Date, venue and timings**, including when you can access the space and when it must be clear.
2. **A floor plan or photos**, with the stage position and audience area.
3. **Audience size** and how people will be arranged.
4. **The programme**: speeches, DJs, live acts, awards, dinner, dance floor.
5. **What the venue already provides**: in-house sound, lighting, power, rigging.
6. **Your priorities and a budget range**, so the proposal can be pitched sensibly.

Then compare quotes on the same basis: what equipment, which crew and for how many hours, what transport, set-up and rehearsal time, and whether taxes such as GST are included.

## Where to save, and where not to

Sensible savings:

- Combine set-up days with the venue's other bookings, or accept an earlier load-in when it is cheaper.
- Match the scale of the system to the audience you actually expect.
- Use the venue's existing infrastructure where it is genuinely suitable.

False economies:

- Cutting tuning or rehearsal time.
- Removing the operator so the system "runs itself".
- Undersizing the system so it runs at its limit all night.
