When a live event goes well, the technical side becomes invisible. Nobody notices the cable that was routed around the fire exit, the generator that was sized for the lighting load, or the cue that was timed to the second. They notice the music, the atmosphere and the moment.

That invisibility is almost always planned. Here is what technical planning involves, and the specific problems each part prevents.

## The cheapest place to solve a problem is on paper

Every problem has a cost, and that cost grows the later it is found. A speaker position that clashes with a doorway takes a minute to move on a drawing, an hour to move on the day, and cannot be moved at all once the audience is in. Planning is simply the practice of finding those problems while they are still cheap.

## The site survey

A visit to the venue, or at least detailed plans and photographs, answers questions that no amount of guessing can:

- Where can equipment be loaded in, and how far does it have to travel?
- Where are the power supplies, and how much do they provide?
- What can be rigged from the ceiling, and what are the load ratings?
- Where are the fire exits, sprinkler heads and smoke detectors?
- What are the venue's rules about noise, haze, load-in times and suppliers?

**Prevents:** equipment that does not fit through the door, insufficient power, rigging that cannot be approved, and late surprises from venue rules.

## Drawings: plan and section

A scaled plan (from above) and section (from the side) put everything in one place: stage, audience, loudspeakers, lighting positions, screens, front of house, cable routes and exits.

**Prevents:** sightline problems, speakers aimed at walls instead of people, lights in front of screens, and clashes between departments who each assumed the same space.

## System design and prediction

For sound, prediction software shows how the system will cover the audience before it is built. For lighting, a plot shows every fixture's position and purpose; visualisation software can preview looks in advance.

**Prevents:** uneven coverage, systems that are too small (or larger than needed), and lighting that looks impressive in isolation but misses the moments that matter.

## Power and signal plans

A power plan lists every load and where it connects, balanced across the supply, with a generator sized correctly where one is needed. A signal flow diagram shows how audio, lighting data and video move between consoles, stage and equipment.

**Prevents:** tripped breakers mid-show, hum and interference, and the hour-long search for why one input has stopped working.

## The run of show and cue sheet

The run of show lists every segment of the event in order, with timings. The cue sheet translates it into technical actions: which microphone opens, which lighting look runs, which video plays, and who calls it.

**Prevents:** missed walk-up music, presenters speaking into a closed microphone, and lighting that changes a beat too late. It also means everyone on the technical team is working from the same document.

## Talking across disciplines

Sound, lighting, video, staging and the venue all depend on each other. A lighting truss affects where speakers can fly. A video wall's position affects where lights can point. Haze affects fire detection. The schedule affects everyone.

Bringing these conversations together early, ideally with one person responsible for the overall technical plan, is the single biggest improvement most events can make.

**Prevents:** departments discovering each other's requirements on site, and compromises made under time pressure.

## The schedule

A realistic production schedule works backwards from doors-open: rehearsals before that, then soundcheck, tuning, focusing and programming, then installation, then load-in. It includes buffers, because something always takes longer than expected.

**Prevents:** rushed tuning, skipped rehearsals and a stressed crew running at the limit just as the audience arrives.

## Risk and contingency

A good plan asks "what if?" for the things that matter most:

- What if the main playback source fails? (A backup is ready and patched.)
- What if a radio microphone drops out? (A spare is on stage.)
- What if it rains on an outdoor event? (Covers, cable protection and a plan for wet ground.)
- What if the power fails? (A clear plan for what goes back on first, and in what order.)

**Prevents:** a single failure becoming the thing people remember about the event.

## What this means for clients

For the person organising an event, technical planning mostly looks like questions: about the venue, the programme, the timings and the priorities. Those questions are not bureaucracy. They are how a production partner turns a brief into a show that runs smoothly.
