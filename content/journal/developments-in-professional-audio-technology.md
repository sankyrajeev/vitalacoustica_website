Professional audio rarely changes overnight. Most real progress arrives as a steady shift in how systems are designed, connected and run, and only becomes obvious when you compare a show today with one from a decade ago. These are the developments we think matter most for live events, and what each means in practice.

## 1. Audio has moved onto the network

Not long ago, a stage was connected to front of house by a thick multicore cable, one pair of wires per channel. Today, most professional systems carry audio over standard network infrastructure.

- **Dante**, from Audinate, has become a common way to move many channels of audio between stage boxes, consoles, processors and amplifiers over Ethernet.
- **AES67** is an open standard for audio-over-IP interoperability, allowing equipment using different networking technologies to exchange audio.
- **Milan**, built on AVB (Audio Video Bridging) and developed through the AVnu Alliance, has been adopted by several loudspeaker and amplifier manufacturers for deterministic, standards-based networking.

**What it means for events:** lighter cabling, faster set-ups and redundant networks that can switch to a backup path if a cable fails. It also means sound systems now need network planning, with proper switches, addressing and a clear understanding of which protocol runs where.

## 2. Bass that goes where it is aimed

Low frequencies naturally spread in every direction, which is why bass often builds up on stage and carries into neighbouring buildings. Directional low-frequency arrays, using cardioid subwoofers or carefully arranged and timed groups, reduce the energy behind and to the sides.

**What it means for events:** a cleaner stage for performers, less spill towards neighbours, and more control in venues with noise limits. It also means sub arrangements are designed, not just placed.

## 3. Prediction before installation

Major loudspeaker manufacturers provide modelling software, such as d&b audiotechnik's ArrayCalc, L-Acoustics' Soundvision and Meyer Sound's MAPP 3D, that predicts how a system will cover an audience area before it is built.

**What it means for events:** coverage, array angles, rigging loads and delay positions can be checked on screen during planning. Fewer surprises on site, and designs that can be shared and discussed with clients and venues before the day.

## 4. Measurement-led tuning

Measurement microphones and analysis software are now standard tools for system engineers. Instead of tuning by ear from one position, engineers measure across the audience area and align subwoofers, mains, fills and delays so the system behaves as one.

**What it means for events:** more consistent sound from front to back, and a set-up process that can be documented and repeated.

## 5. Immersive and object-based systems

Several manufacturers now offer systems that place sounds in space, rather than simply sending a stereo mix left and right. Examples include d&b audiotechnik's Soundscape, L-Acoustics' L-ISA and Meyer Sound's Spacemap Go. Companies such as Holoplot use beam-forming arrays to steer sound to specific areas of a room.

**What it means for events:** better localisation (voices that appear to come from the person speaking), more even coverage for wide audiences, and new creative possibilities for performances and installations. These systems need more loudspeakers, more planning and specialist operation, so they suit some events far better than others.

## 6. More power in less weight

Amplifiers have become more efficient and more powerful for their size, with processing and network control built in. Loudspeakers have become lighter for their output.

**What it means for events:** smaller truck loads, faster rigging and lower power demand, which matters both for cost and for events running on generators.

## 7. Sustainability is on the brief

Touring productions and festivals increasingly measure the environmental impact of their events. For audio, that means transport efficiency, power consumption, equipment lifespan and repairability.

**What it means for events:** right-sizing systems, using efficient amplification, and planning logistics carefully are becoming part of what clients expect from a production partner.

## What to take from this

Most of these developments point the same way: away from improvisation and towards systems that are designed, predicted, measured and connected. For anyone planning an event, the practical question to ask a provider is not "which brand do you use?" but "how do you design, verify and run the system?"
