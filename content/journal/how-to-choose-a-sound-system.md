The most common way to choose a sound system is to start with a list of speakers. It is also the least reliable. Two venues with the same capacity can need completely different systems, because the thing you are really designing for is not the headcount. It is the room, the people in it, and what they have come to hear.

Here is how we would think it through, in the order that matters.

## 1. Start with the room

Before anyone talks about brands or boxes, get the basic geometry down on paper:

- **Length, width and ceiling height.** A long, narrow hall behaves very differently from a wide, shallow one, even at the same floor area.
- **Where the audience will actually be.** Standing in front of a DJ, seated at round tables, spread across a lawn: each changes where the sound needs to go.
- **The surfaces.** Glass, stone and bare concrete reflect; curtains, carpets and people absorb. A hard, empty room will sound very different at soundcheck than it does full.
- **Indoors or outdoors.** Outdoors there are no walls to help or hurt, but wind, temperature and neighbours all start to matter.

A simple plan and section drawing, even a sketch with measurements, is worth more at this stage than any specification sheet.

## 2. Know what the system is for

A system that is excellent for a keynote can be wrong for a club night, and the other way round.

- **Speech** needs clarity and even coverage more than volume. Every seat should hear the presenter at a similar level, without the front rows being blasted.
- **Recorded music and DJ sets** need clean, extended low end and enough headroom to stay comfortable at the levels people expect.
- **Live bands** add the stage: monitors, microphones close to loud instruments, and a sound engineer who needs to hear what the audience hears.

Write down the programme for the day, hour by hour. Many events are all three at once: a welcome speech, a dinner, then a dance floor.

## 3. Coverage: everyone should hear the same show

The goal is not "loud enough at the front". It is a consistent experience from the front row to the back wall. A single speaker loses about 6 dB every time the distance doubles, so someone 4 metres away hears it far louder than someone at 16 metres. That is why larger rooms use:

- **Arrays** that are shaped to spread energy more evenly from front to back.
- **Front fills** for the first rows, which the main speakers fly over.
- **Delay speakers** further back, timed so that their sound arrives with the main system rather than ahead of it.

A good provider will show you how the system covers the audience area, ideally with a prediction from the manufacturer's modelling software, before anything is loaded onto a truck. If you would like to see why this matters, our guide to [line arrays and point-source speakers](/journal/line-array-vs-point-source) goes into the trade-offs.

## 4. Low end is its own decision

Subwoofers decide how a dance floor feels, and they are also what travels furthest through walls and into neighbouring buildings. Three questions are worth asking:

- How much low end does the programme actually need? A jazz brunch and a techno night are not the same brief.
- Where will the subs go? Their placement changes where bass builds up and where it cancels.
- Does the low end need to be kept off the stage or away from a boundary? Cardioid and end-fire arrangements can steer bass forward and reduce it behind, which helps performers and neighbours alike.

## 5. Headroom: why a bigger system can sound quieter

A system running near its limit distorts, compresses and becomes tiring to listen to, even if the meter says the level is reasonable. A system with headroom delivers the same level cleanly, with peaks that still have room to land. If two quotes differ mainly in the number of boxes, this is often the reason.

## 6. Where it can go, and how it is held up

The best design on paper still has to be installed safely:

- **Flown** systems need rigging points rated for the load, and someone qualified to use them.
- **Ground-stacked** systems need stable platforms and clear sightlines.
- Speaker stands, cable runs and amplifier positions all need space that does not block exits or create trip hazards.

Ask the venue early for its rigging plan, load ratings and any rules about what can be attached where.

## 7. Power and noise

Professional audio needs clean, dedicated power. Find out what the venue provides, where the supply is, and whether a generator will be needed. A short conversation here prevents hum, tripped breakers and unpleasant surprises during the show.

Noise rules matter too. In India, the Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000 restrict loudspeaker use at night and set ambient limits for different zones, and outdoor events commonly need police permission. Venues often add their own curfews and limits. Design for the level you are allowed to run, not the level you would like.

## 8. Questions to ask any provider

- Can you show me how the system covers my audience area?
- Who will set up and tune the system, and will they measure it in the room?
- Will an engineer be operating it during the event, or is it left to run?
- What is the plan if something fails mid-show?
- What do you need from the venue: power, access, rigging, load-in times?

The answers tell you more than the brand names on the quote.

## A short checklist

1. A plan of the venue with measurements, and where the audience will be.
2. The programme: speech, recorded music, live performance, or all three.
3. Audience size and how it changes through the day.
4. Rigging and power information from the venue.
5. Any noise limits, curfews and permissions.
6. Load-in and load-out times.
