Corporate events ask lighting to do several jobs at once. It has to make presenters clearly visible, look right on camera, carry the brand, and change the mood of the room as the day moves from keynote to dinner to party. None of that is difficult, but it does need to be planned in the right order.

## Start with the run of show

Before choosing a single fixture, write down what happens in the room, in order:

- Doors and walk-in
- Welcome and keynote
- Panels or presentations
- Awards or announcements
- Dinner
- Entertainment or a dance floor

Each segment is a **lighting state**: a look for the room. Planning from the programme means the rig is built to deliver those states, rather than the programme being squeezed into whatever the rig can do.

## Light the people first

The most important lighting at a corporate event is the least glamorous: front light on the presenters.

- **Light from two sides.** A common approach is two front-light sources for each position, roughly 45 degrees to either side and 30 to 45 degrees above the presenter's eye line. It lights the face evenly without flattening it.
- **Avoid the lectern trap.** Presenters often wander. Light the whole area they might use, not just the lectern.
- **Keep it comfortable.** Bright enough for cameras, but not so bright that presenters cannot see the audience or read their notes.
- **Do not forget the panel.** Seated panellists need light too, from a lower angle, so faces are not lost under brows.

## Design for the camera

Most corporate events are filmed, streamed or shown on large screens (image magnification, or IMAG). Cameras are less forgiving than eyes:

- **Keep colour temperature consistent.** Mixing warm and cool white sources on faces looks fine in the room and wrong on camera. Choose one and match the fixtures to it.
- **Choose fixtures that render skin well.** Cheaper LEDs can make faces look green or grey. High colour-rendering fixtures avoid this.
- **Check for flicker.** Some LED fixtures pulse at frequencies a camera can see as banding or strobing. Professional fixtures usually offer flicker-free or high-frequency modes; test with the actual cameras.
- **Balance the screens.** LED walls and projection are often much brighter than the stage. Presenters standing in front of them need enough light to match, or they become silhouettes on the stream.
- **Keep light off projection screens.** Stray light washes out projected images.

## Bring in the brand, carefully

Brand colours are where corporate lighting is most noticeable, and where it most often goes slightly wrong.

- **Test the colours.** A brand's exact colour may not be reproduced perfectly by every LED fixture. Check it in the room, and on camera, before the day.
- **Use colour on surfaces, not faces.** Wash the set, the walls, drapes or architecture in brand colour, and keep presenters in clean white light.
- **Use gobos and projection sparingly.** A logo projected on a wall or floor is effective once. Repeated everywhere, it starts to look like signage.

## Change the room with the programme

The same room can feel like a conference hall at 10 am and a party at 10 pm. That transformation is a large part of the value of lighting:

- **Walk-in:** a welcoming ambient look, with the stage and brand elements lit.
- **Keynote:** focus on the stage, house lights lowered but not dark, so the audience can still take notes.
- **Awards:** dedicated looks for walk-ups and announcements, often with a short build in intensity or movement.
- **Dinner:** warm, lower, flattering light on tables.
- **Party:** movement, colour and effects, with the stage and dance floor as the focus.

Programming these as recalled cues means each change is consistent, quick and timed to the moment, rather than adjusted by hand during the event.

## Work within the venue

Hotels and convention centres have their own constraints. Ask early about:

- **Rigging:** ceiling height, rated points and whether you can fly truss or must ground-support it.
- **Power:** what is available, where, and whether additional power is needed.
- **Load-in windows:** many venues allow only a short slot before the event, which affects how much can be rigged and focused.
- **Haze and smoke:** atmospheric effects can trigger fire detection. Venues often require permission and a plan for detectors before any haze is used.
- **House lights:** whether they can be controlled from the lighting console, and who operates them.

## Keep it safe

- Exits and walkways must stay visible at all times, including during dark moments in the show.
- Cables need to be routed, covered and taped so no one trips.
- Fixtures above people need safety bonds, and rigging must be done by qualified crew.

## Leave time to focus and rehearse

The difference between an adequate and an excellent corporate event is often the rehearsal. Focusing lights on presenter positions, testing looks on camera, checking brand colours and running the cues with the show caller all take time. Build it into the schedule, ideally with the presenters, or at least stand-ins, on stage.

## A planning checklist

1. The run of show, segment by segment.
2. A stage plan with presenter positions, lectern and panel seating.
3. Camera positions and whether the event is streamed or shown on IMAG.
4. Brand colours, with references.
5. Venue information: rigging, power, load-in times and rules on haze.
6. Rehearsal time with the people who will be on stage.
